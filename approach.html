Our approach

Rooted in science. Led by curiosity.

Our work draws from several fields, and we are careful about what each practice means and when it is appropriate.

Five sources of practice

Different methods, one respect for people and nature.

Four fields inspire our programme design. Therapeutic horticulture is a practice our certified team members can deliver directly. Each format is selected with the participant group and its goals in mind.

01

Forest School-inspired practice

Learner choice, regular contact and supported risk

Forest School places value on extended, regular experiences, learner-led play, reflection, relationships and suitable challenge. We draw on its child-centred methods when appropriate. A one-off workshop is not described as a full Forest School programme.

Read the reference
02

Forest therapy-inspired practice

Sensory attention and gentle invitations

Gentle invitations to notice sounds, textures, movement and the present moment can help participants spend intentional time in nature. This does not mean guides provide psychological therapy.

Read the reference
03

Nature-based intervention-inspired practice

Using green spaces to support defined aims

A nature-based activity can be planned around social participation, learning, attention or wellbeing. Goals and outcomes should be appropriate to the setting, group and qualifications of practitioners.

Read the reference
04

Therapeutic horticulture

Wellbeing through plant-related activity

Our team includes practitioners certified in therapeutic horticulture. We plan plant-centred experiences to support meaningful participation, wellbeing, social engagement and everyday skills. Where clinical horticultural therapy or a formal treatment plan is required, the service and practitioner scope must be assessed separately.

Read the reference
05

Recreational therapy-inspired practice

Meaningful participation and functional goals

Recreation can be used to support engagement and practical abilities. Formal recreational therapy involves assessment and qualified practice. We describe our work as recreation-informed unless delivered under the appropriate clinical scope.

Read the reference
How we facilitate

An invitation, not a lecture.

We use open questions, observation and the freedom to engage differently. A facilitator might ask, "What do you notice?", "What do you think happens next?" or "What could we try?"

Depending on the group, sessions may be more child-led, guided or structured. These choices are purposeful: a safety-critical task may need direct instruction, while an observation activity may benefit from free exploration.

01

Inquiry-based learning

Look closely, pose questions, make predictions and test ideas.

02

Participant-led choice

Offer options, flexible pacing and room to follow interests.

03

Supported participation

Adapt activities and assistance without taking over what a participant can do independently.

04

Reflective practice

Invite people to connect what happened with their own lives, learning or relationships.

Professional boundaries: Second Nature's community education and wellbeing programmes should not be presented as medical, psychological or rehabilitation treatment by default. Where clinical treatment is requested, this requires appropriate licensed or otherwise qualified professionals, defined goals and procedures.
